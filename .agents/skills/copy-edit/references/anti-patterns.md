# AI-Writing Anti-Patterns

A practical editorial reference for recognizing and removing predictable AI-writing behaviors that weaken prose, alter the source voice, flatten reasoning, inflate claims, or make the writing feel generic.

## How to use this guide

These are recurring tendencies, not forbidden words. A pattern should be removed only when it weakens clarity, specificity, rhythm, reasoning, or voice.

The editor should never deliberately introduce awkwardness, errors, slang, or random variation merely to make prose seem human. Detection tools and public AI-word lists should not be treated as authorities. The source text and approved examples remain the strongest guides.

## 1. Unnecessary rewriting

### Pattern

The tendency to alter clear, natural sentences merely because the model was asked to edit.

### Common symptoms

- Replacing ordinary words with synonyms
- Reordering a sentence without improving it
- Increasing formality
- Removing distinctive phrasing
- Changing rhythm while preserving essentially the same meaning
- Editing every sentence regardless of need

### Editorial test

- What specific problem does this change solve?
- Is the revision more precise, clearer, or more economical?
- Does the source sentence already work?

### Revision approach

Leave effective sentences intact. Require every substantial change to earn its place.

### Do not overcorrect

A sentence that is clear, natural, and distinctive should remain unchanged. The goal is not to make every sentence the best possible version of itself.

## 2. Generic polished voice

### Pattern

Prose that is grammatically smooth but no longer sounds specific to the source.

### Common symptoms

- Formal but impersonal vocabulary
- Uniformly polished sentences
- Abstract professional language
- Removal of contractions
- Removal of understated humor
- Replacing observation with explanation
- Writing that could plausibly belong to almost anyone

### Editorial test

- Could this paragraph have been generated for a different writer or subject with only a few nouns changed?
- Did the revision preserve distinctive wording and point of view?

### Revision approach

Restore specific observations, the original degree of formality, and wording that reflects the source voice. If the paragraph reads like it could belong to anyone, it needs another pass.

### Do not overcorrect

Not every polished sentence is generic. Some source prose is naturally direct and clean. The problem is uniformity that replaces the writer's rhythm, not clarity itself.

## 3. Formulaic openings

### Pattern

Opening sentences that delay the subject with boilerplate framing.

### Common symptoms

- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape"
- "In an increasingly complex world"
- "At its core"
- "When it comes to"
- "It is important to note"
- "It is worth considering"
- "There is no denying"
- "As technology continues to evolve"

### Editorial test

- Does the opening introduce the subject immediately?
- Does it delay the reader with throat-clearing?

### Revision approach

Begin with the concrete observation, event, claim, or tension. Cut framing that does not carry specific meaning.

### Do not overcorrect

A brief contextual opening is not automatically formulaic. Evaluate whether it introduces useful framing or merely delays the subject.

## 4. Decorative transitions

### Pattern

Transitions that imply a logical relationship the prose has not established.

### Common symptoms

- Additionally
- Moreover
- Furthermore
- Consequently
- Ultimately
- Importantly
- Notably
- With that said
- That being said

### Editorial test

- Is the relationship additive, causal, contrasting, chronological, qualifying, or merely adjacent?
- Can the relationship be named precisely?
- Would the paragraph read better without the transition?

### Revision approach

Name the relationship or remove the transition. A paragraph that follows naturally from the previous one does not need an explicit connector.

### Do not overcorrect

Do not ban these words when they accurately express the relationship. "Additionally" is fine when listing a genuinely distinct point. "Consequently" is fine when describing a clear result.

## 5. Manufactured contrasts

### Pattern

Creating an artificial binary because contrast sounds analytical.

### Common symptoms

- "Not just X, but Y"
- "It is not X; it is Y"
- "Less about X and more about Y"
- "The question is not whether X, but how Y"
- "X is not merely a tool; it is…"
- "Rather than X, we should Y"
- "The real challenge is…"

### Editorial test

- Are X and Y genuinely opposed?
- Did the source establish this contrast?
- Does the formulation clarify a real distinction or manufacture significance?

### Revision approach

Preserve genuine distinctions, especially where technical precision depends on them. Remove contrasts that impose a binary where the source expresses continuity or coexistence.

### Do not overcorrect

Some of these constructions reflect real technical distinctions that the writer intentionally drew. Do not collapse a genuine contrast.

## 6. Lists of three

### Pattern

The automatic preference for three adjectives, examples, clauses, or benefits.

### Common symptoms

- Three parallel adjectives in repeated sentences
- Every paragraph containing a three-part list
- Adding a weak third item to complete a rhythm
- Repeated "clear, scalable, and maintainable" style constructions

### Editorial test

- Are all three items necessary and distinct?
- Was the final item added only for cadence?

### Revision approach

Reduce to the number of items the content actually requires. Two specific examples are better than three where the third is filler.

### Do not overcorrect

Do not remove useful three-part lists merely because they contain three items. Some ideas genuinely have three components.

## 7. Uniform sentence rhythm

### Pattern

Prose where sentence lengths, clause structures, and openings become too regular.

### Common symptoms

- Several sentences of nearly identical length
- Repeated subject-verb openings
- Every sentence containing two balanced clauses
- Repeated "This means…" or "This creates…" openings
- A steady cadence without natural emphasis or interruption

### Revision approach

Restore natural variation. Preserve occasional short sentences for emphasis. Keep longer sentences where they are carrying connected reasoning. Do not create random variation for its own sake.

### Do not overcorrect

Not every paragraph needs maximal sentence-length diversity. A technical explanation may naturally use similar sentence structures for parallel steps.

## 8. Fragmented paragraph rhythm

### Pattern

Splitting coherent prose into a staircase of short or one-sentence paragraphs.

### Common symptoms

```text
The tools were useful.

But they were inconsistent.

That changed the workflow.

Context mattered.
```

### Editorial test

- Do these sentences develop the same stage of thought?
- Would a connected paragraph read more naturally?

### Revision approach

Reconnect sentences that develop the same stage of thought. Preserve paragraph-level reasoning. Use isolated sentences only for genuine emphasis, pause, or transition.

### Do not overcorrect

Short paragraphs are valid for emphasis, transition, or introducing a new point. The problem is fragmentation that breaks a single thought into pieces.

## 9. Modular paragraphs

### Pattern

Paragraphs that are individually competent but interchangeable.

### Common symptoms

- Each paragraph starts with a topic sentence and ends with a takeaway
- Paragraphs could be reordered without changing the argument
- Every paragraph follows claim, explanation, implication
- Paragraphs repeat the same idea at increasing levels of abstraction
- Transitions connect blocks rather than reasoning

### Editorial test

- Does each paragraph change what the next paragraph can say?
- Is there a causal, chronological, or conceptual progression?
- Is the piece developing an argument or stacking content modules?

### Revision approach

Restore dependency between paragraphs. Preserve the sequence by which the author reaches the conclusion.

### Do not overcorrect

Some subjects benefit from parallel structure. Comparison pieces, for example, may legitimately use modular paragraphs. The test is whether the structure serves the argument or replaces it.

## 10. Excessive signposting

### Pattern

Announcing what the prose is about to do, when the prose already speaks clearly.

### Common symptoms

- "It is important to understand"
- "It is worth noting"
- "To put this into perspective"
- "In other words"
- "What this means is"
- "The key distinction is"
- "The takeaway here is"
- "As we have seen"
- "This brings us to"

### Editorial test

- Does the signpost guide the reader through a real turn?
- Does it merely announce what the next sentence already says?
- Can the underlying claim begin directly?

### Revision approach

Cut signposts that state the obvious. Keep them only when the reader genuinely needs advance notice of a shift.

### Do not overcorrect

Some signposts are necessary for clarity, especially in longer or more complex pieces. Keep what earns its place.

## 11. Repetition disguised as clarification

### Pattern

Stating an idea, paraphrasing it, then summarizing it.

### Common symptoms

- A clear sentence followed by "In other words"
- Multiple sentences expressing the same claim at different abstraction levels
- Repeating a paragraph's opening in its closing sentence
- Repeating the conclusion after every example

### Revision approach

Keep the strongest formulation. Retain clarification only when it adds a distinction, example, consequence, or necessary simplification.

### Do not overcorrect

Some repetition is intentional — the writer may be building a case through restatement. Distinguish purposeful emphasis from redundant restatement.

## 12. Over-explanation

### Pattern

Explaining concepts the intended audience likely understands.

### Common symptoms

- Defining common technical terms unnecessarily
- Explaining a metaphor immediately after using it
- Adding basic background that interrupts the argument
- Walking the reader through an obvious inference
- Explaining why an example matters when the connection is already clear

### Editorial test

- Who is the intended audience?
- Does this explanation resolve a likely misunderstanding?
- Does it add information or merely restate the point?

### Revision approach

Trust the audience. Remove explanations that the reader does not need.

### Do not overcorrect

When the audience is mixed or the concept is genuinely obscure, an explanation may be necessary. The question is whether the explanation carries its weight.

## 13. Inflated importance

### Pattern

Emphasis unsupported by evidence.

### Common symptoms

- Crucial
- Critical
- Profound
- Powerful
- Transformative
- Fundamental
- Remarkable
- Compelling
- Meaningful
- Significant
- Groundbreaking
- Game-changing
- More important than ever
- The stakes could not be higher
- A powerful testament
- "This underscores…"
- "This highlights…"
- "This demonstrates…"
- "This reveals…"

### Editorial test

- Does the evidence prove, demonstrate, suggest, or merely illustrate?
- Can the importance be shown through a concrete consequence?
- Is the adjective performing work the argument should perform?

### Revision approach

State what happened and let the reader evaluate its importance. If the significance is not obvious, show it through consequence rather than declaration.

### Do not overcorrect

Genuinely important claims deserve appropriate emphasis. The problem is inflation, not emphasis itself.

## 14. Generic conclusions

### Pattern

Endings that seal the piece with a reusable lesson rather than a specific reflection.

### Common symptoms

- "The future is bright"
- "Only time will tell"
- "At the end of the day"
- "This serves as a reminder"
- "The key takeaway"
- "Ultimately, success depends on…"
- "By embracing these principles…"
- "As we move forward…"
- "The journey has only just begun"

### Editorial test

- Does this conclusion arise from the specific exploration in the piece?
- Could it conclude a different article on a different subject?

### Revision approach

End with the clearest implication, unresolved question, practical consequence, or changed understanding already earned by the text. Do not add a conclusion when the piece works better without one.

### Do not overcorrect

Some conclusions are naturally forward-looking. The problem is a formula that could preface any topic.

## 15. Generic humanistic framing

### Pattern

Human concerns stated as abstractions rather than grounded in concrete experience.

### Common symptoms

- "At the end of the day, this is about people"
- "Technology should serve humanity"
- "Behind every data point is a person"
- "The human element"
- "A deeply human challenge"
- "What truly matters"
- "Empowering people"
- "Building a better future"
- "Human-centered" without a specific design consequence

### Editorial test

- Is there a concrete experience, tradeoff, decision, or consequence behind this framing?
- Would the same sentence appear in a corporate mission statement?

### Revision approach

Ground human concerns in a specific encounter or decision. Show what "human-centered" means in practice.

### Do not overcorrect

Do not remove genuine human observations merely because similar language can be clichéd. Test whether the observation is specific to this piece.

## 16. False certainty

### Pattern

Emphasis that outruns the evidence.

### Common symptoms

- Clearly
- Obviously
- Undoubtedly
- This proves
- There is no question
- The answer is simple
- Always
- Never (when the evidence does not support it)

### Editorial test

- Is the claim as certain as the word suggests?
- Would a qualified statement be more accurate?

### Revision approach

Calibrate certainty to evidence. Preserve uncertainty where the source is exploring, interpreting, or qualifying a claim.

### Do not overcorrect

Genuinely settled facts can be stated directly. The problem is overstatement, not confidence.

## 17. Empty caution

### Pattern

Excessive hedging that adds no useful precision.

### Common symptoms

- May potentially
- Could possibly
- In some ways
- To some extent
- In many cases (without identifying which cases)
- It depends (without naming dependencies)
- Generic caveats appended to every claim

### Editorial test

- What uncertainty is being expressed?
- Can the condition or dependency be named?
- Is the qualifier necessary for accuracy?

### Revision approach

Name the condition or remove the hedge. Preserve meaningful qualification.

### Do not overcorrect

Necessary qualification protects accuracy. Remove hedging only when it is reflexive rather than precise.

## 18. Abstract nominalization

### Pattern

Converting direct actions into noun-heavy prose.

### Common symptoms

- "We decided" becoming "A decision was made"
- "The tools behaved differently" becoming "Variability in tool behavior was observed"
- Frequent use of: implementation of, utilization of, facilitation of, optimization of, consideration of, execution of, evaluation of

### Editorial test

- Does the abstract form improve precision or reduce it?
- Does the nominalization distance the reader from the action?

### Revision approach

Prefer clear subjects and direct verbs when they improve readability. Retain technical nouns when they name real concepts.

### Do not overcorrect

Some nominalizations are standard terminology. "Implementation" is often the right word. The problem is converting every verb into a noun.

## 19. Passive voice without purpose

### Pattern

Passive constructions that weaken observation or conceal responsibility.

### Common symptoms

- "It was determined that…"
- "It was observed…" (when the writer is the observer)
- Passive constructions that increase word count without adding clarity

### Editorial test

- Does the passive voice serve a purpose (unknown actor, focus on result, technical convention)?
- Would the active voice be clearer and more direct?

### Revision approach

Use active voice unless the passive serves a specific purpose.

### Do not overcorrect

Passive voice is not inherently wrong. Retain it when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, the action or result deserves emphasis, or technical convention makes it clearer.

## 20. Generic abstraction

### Pattern

Replacing specific observations with broad concepts.

### Common symptoms

- "The tool returned different results" becoming "This reflects the complexity of modern AI systems"
- "The team could not maintain it" becoming "Scalability and maintainability became key considerations"
- Moving from concrete event to abstract principle without preserving the event

### Editorial test

- Did the revision remove the concrete event?
- Is the abstraction supported by an example?
- Could the broad sentence apply to almost any subject?

### Revision approach

Keep the specific observation. Let the abstraction follow from it rather than replace it.

### Do not overcorrect

Generalization is sometimes necessary. The problem is losing the concrete foundation, not the generalization itself.

## 21. Vocabulary clouds

### Pattern

Repeated use of topic-associated words that make prose feel statistically assembled.

### Common symptoms

Clusters of words such as:

- landscape, ecosystem, dynamic, evolving
- robust, seamless, holistic, nuanced, multifaceted
- paradigm, intersection, realm, journey, tapestry
- unlock, harness, empower, leverage, foster, elevate
- navigate, delve

### Editorial test

- Do these words accumulate in a way that draws attention to themselves?
- Do they replace concrete description?
- Would a plainer word serve better?

### Revision approach

Replace vocabulary-cloud words with concrete terms when they are doing ornamental work. Repeat the correct technical term instead of cycling through near-synonyms.

### Do not overcorrect

Do not ban these words individually. Most have legitimate uses. Flag them when they accumulate, replace concrete description, or drift outside the source writer's normal vocabulary.

## 22. Unnecessary synonym replacement

### Pattern

Avoiding repetition of precise terms by substituting near-synonyms.

### Common symptoms

- Switching between terms that have distinct meanings as if they were interchangeable
- Varying terminology for the sake of variety rather than precision
- Blurring distinctions the source carefully maintained

### Examples of distinctions that must not be blurred

- instruction and context
- ambiguity and inconsistency
- deterministic and predictable
- capability and behavior
- interface and experience
- implementation and architecture

### Revision approach

Repeat the precise term when consistency matters. Use variation only when the words are truly interchangeable.

### Do not overcorrect

Some variation is natural and improves readability. The problem is variation that sacrifices precision.

## 23. Artificial completeness

### Pattern

Making every piece exhaustive regardless of scope.

### Common symptoms

- Adding benefits, risks, challenges, and opportunities to a focused observation
- Turning a narrow observation into a comprehensive framework
- Adding edge cases without relevance
- Creating unnecessary taxonomies
- Filling intentional gaps
- Adding future implications
- Adding a summary section because it seems expected

### Editorial test

- Does the addition serve the central argument?
- Did the author ask for comprehensiveness?
- Does the extra material dilute the strongest point?

### Revision approach

Keep the piece focused. A narrow observation that is fully explored is more useful than a broad framework that is thinly covered.

### Do not overcorrect

Some pieces genuinely benefit from exploring implications or edge cases. The test is whether the addition earns its space.

## 24. Invented implications

### Pattern

Conclusions or consequences not present in the source.

### Common symptoms

- Claiming organizational impact without evidence
- Adding business value to a technical observation
- Adding social significance
- Turning a personal experience into a universal rule
- Adding recommendations the author did not make

### Editorial test

- Does the source support this implication?
- Is this a reasonable inference or an invention?

### Revision approach

Do not expand the claim beyond what the source supports. Flag possible implications separately rather than silently inserting them.

### Do not overcorrect

Some reasonable inferences can be drawn from the source. The problem is claiming implications the author did not establish.

## 25. Invented personal experience

### Prohibition

- Do not add autobiographical details.
- Do not fabricate feelings.
- Do not claim the author tried, learned, noticed, or believed something not present in the source.
- Do not invent anecdotes to make prose warmer.
- Do not add quotations attributed to the author.

The editor may strengthen the expression of an experience already present but must not create one.

## 26. Unnecessary anthropomorphism

### Pattern

Attributing human understanding, intention, confidence, desire, or judgment to software systems.

### Common symptoms

- "The model knows"
- "The agent wants"
- "The tool understands"
- "The system decides" when it is actually following a rule or producing a probabilistic output

### Editorial test

- Is the anthropomorphic wording clearly figurative?
- Is the author deliberately examining anthropomorphism?
- Does the wording support understated humor?
- Would replacing it make the prose awkward without improving precision?

### Revision approach

Use precise language about what systems do. Reserve human verbs for human agents.

### Do not overcorrect

Retain anthropomorphic wording when it is clearly figurative, the author is deliberately examining anthropomorphism, it supports understated humor, or replacing it would make the prose awkward without improving precision.

## 27. Decorative punctuation

### Pattern

Punctuation used for effect rather than structure.

### Common symptoms

- Excessive em dashes
- Semicolons added to create sophistication
- Colons introducing trivial statements
- Parenthetical asides added repeatedly
- Quotation marks used for vague emphasis
- Bold text used to manufacture importance

### Revision approach

Use punctuation to express real structure. Preserve simple punctuation when it is sufficient. Do not remove purposeful punctuation merely to enforce uniformity.

### Do not overcorrect

The source may use em dashes, semicolons, or parentheticals purposefully. Preserve intentional punctuation choices.

## 28. Headings that narrate instead of organize

### Pattern

Headings that summarize content rather than helping the reader navigate.

### Common symptoms

- A heading for every short paragraph
- Generic headings such as "The Challenge," "The Solution," and "The Future"
- Headings that repeat the first sentence
- Turning reflective prose into a report template
- Adding headings when the piece already has clear progression

### Editorial test

- Does the heading help the reader navigate?
- Does it divide a meaningful section?
- Would the prose be stronger without it?

### Revision approach

Use headings only when they organize distinct sections. Do not add headings to prose that flows naturally without them.

### Do not overcorrect

Some pieces benefit from clear section markers. The problem is headings that substitute for structure rather than reflecting it.

## 29. Overuse of rhetorical questions

### Pattern

Creating engagement through repeated questions.

### Common symptoms

- Asking a question and answering it immediately
- Using questions to introduce obvious claims
- Several paragraphs beginning with questions
- Questions that manufacture suspense

### Editorial test

- Does the question reflect a genuine unresolved issue?
- Does it mark a meaningful turn in reasoning?

### Revision approach

Retain rhetorical questions when they reflect a real issue or turn. Remove them when they manufacture engagement the content does not need.

### Do not overcorrect

A well-placed rhetorical question can be effective. The problem is frequency and artificiality.

## 30. Generic calls to action

### Pattern

Recommendations that do not follow from the argument.

### Common symptoms

- "It is time to…"
- "We must embrace…"
- "The next step is clear"
- "Organizations should prioritize…"
- "Now is the time"
- "By taking action today…"

### Editorial test

- Does this recommendation follow from the specific argument of the piece?
- Does it use the degree of urgency the evidence supports?

### Revision approach

State the recommendation only when the argument has already earned it. Calibrate urgency to evidence.

### Do not overcorrect

Some pieces genuinely call for action. The problem is a generic appeal that could conclude any persuasive piece.

## 31. Over-finishing

### Pattern

Making prose more polished, conclusive, or symmetrical than the source warrants.

### Common symptoms

- Making every transition explicit
- Making every argument symmetrical
- Resolving every tension
- Adding a conclusion merely for completeness
- Turning every observation into a framework
- Polishing away useful irregularity
- Making a draft more formal than necessary

### Editorial test

- Does the piece feel sealed against further revision?
- Would a reader assume this text is final?
- Does the polish serve clarity or merely appearance?

### Revision approach

Leave room for the writer's final judgment. Preserve loose ends the writer may want to develop differently. Do not impose structure where the writer chose openness.

### Do not overcorrect

Some prose genuinely benefits from a clean finish. The problem is finishing work the writer did not intend to end.

## 32. Premature resolution

### Pattern

Resolving tension, uncertainty, or ambiguity that the writer intentionally left open.

### Common symptoms

- Supplying an answer to a question the writer posed but did not answer
- Resolving a genuine conflict with a compromise formulation
- Smoothing over a contradiction the writer was exploring
- Adding a tidy takeaway after an exploratory passage

### Editorial test

- Did the source indicate this was settled?
- Is the writer exploring rather than concluding?
- Does the resolution shut down a productive line of inquiry?

### Revision approach

Preserve genuine uncertainty. Let unresolved questions remain unresolved. If a gap is material, flag it rather than filling it.

### Do not overcorrect

Some questions are rhetorical. Some tensions exist only to be resolved. The problem is resolving what the writer left open.

## 33. Genre flattening

### Pattern

Making different genres sound identical by applying the same voice template without adaptation.

### Common symptoms

- Technical documentation that reads like a reflective article
- Website copy that sounds detached rather than direct
- Personal notes that are as polished as published prose
- Instructions that include reflection or framing rather than operational steps
- Resume language that drifts into article-like narrative

### Editorial test

- Does the genre require different treatment?
- Would a reader recognize the genre from the prose?
- Does the draft match the conventions of the form while using the house voice?

### Revision approach

Adapt the house voice to the genre. A reflective voice can still be concise in documentation. A direct voice can still be measured in personal writing.

### Do not overcorrect

The voice should be recognizable across genres. The problem is erasing genre distinctions, not maintaining a consistent voice.

## 34. Imported subject matter

### Pattern

Inserting topics, examples, opinions, or personal experiences from unrelated writing samples into new work.

### Common symptoms

- Recurring technical topics that appear across different pieces
- Metaphors or examples that belong to a different article
- Anecdotes or personal experiences the writer did not supply
- Opinions the writer has expressed elsewhere but not in the current material
- Terminology or project names from unrelated writing

### Editorial test

- Does this example come from the current material?
- Is this a topic the writer introduced in this piece?
- Would this passage feel familiar if seen in another context?

### Revision approach

Draw examples and material only from the supplied context. Do not import recurring themes from unrelated samples.

### Do not overcorrect

Some topics naturally recur across a writer's work. The problem is importing matter from unrelated drafts without supply.

## 35. Restarting instead of continuing

### Pattern

When asked to continue an existing draft, beginning again rather than advancing the argument.

### Common symptoms

- The continuation re-establishes context already established
- The continuation summarizes what the preceding paragraph already said
- A new paragraph begins at the same conceptual starting point
- The continuation could be placed anywhere in the piece

### Editorial test

- Does this passage advance the preceding thought?
- Does it assume the reader has already read what comes before?
- Could it be moved to the introduction without change?

### Revision approach

Read the preceding paragraphs closely. Continue the argument as if the reader has already followed it to that point. Advance, do not restate.

### Do not overcorrect

Some continuations need a brief re-anchor after a long gap. The problem is restarting when the argument is already underway.

## 36. Revision distance exceeded

### Pattern

Performing a full rewrite when a lighter edit was requested.

### Common symptoms
- Copy-edit request produces a structural revision
- Tighten request produces a full rewrite
- Minor polish substantially changes sentence rhythm and vocabulary
- Every request produces the same level of intervention regardless of what was asked

### Editorial test

- Does the amount of change match the request?
- Were effective sentences left alone?
- Would a lighter pass have satisfied the stated goal?

### Revision approach

Match intervention to request. When in doubt, make the smallest change that might suffice. The writer can always ask for more.

### Do not overcorrect

Some requests implicitly require more intervention than they state. If a sentence is genuinely unclear, correcting it may require more than surface polish.

## 37. Forced humor

### Pattern

Humor added to make prose seem lively, human, clever, or distinctive even though the source and surrounding passage do not create a natural opening for it.

### Common symptoms

- A joke inserted after a technical statement
- Exaggerated metaphors
- Extended comedic comparisons
- A punchline at the end of each paragraph
- Sudden internet slang
- Sarcasm that changes the writer's posture
- Anthropomorphism added solely for humor
- Self-deprecation not supported by the source
- Invented anecdotes or reactions
- Jokes that must be explained
- Humor that could be removed without changing anything except tone
- Repeated attempts to make tools or agents sound like quirky characters
- A clever phrase that obscures the actual point
- Tonal whiplash between reflective and comedic prose

### Editorial test

- What purpose does the humor serve?
- Did the material naturally suggest it?
- Is it grounded in a real observation?
- Does it preserve the writer's intended tone?
- Does it sharpen recognition or merely seek attention?
- Is the technical meaning still precise?
- Was any experience or reaction invented?
- Does the passage improve when the line is removed?

### Revision approach

- Remove decorative jokes.
- Return to the concrete observation.
- Replace exaggeration with understatement.
- Shorten an extended metaphor to a brief aside when appropriate.
- Preserve the humorous line only if it supports recognition, rhythm, or perspective.
- Keep the argument primary.

### Do not overcorrect

Humor should not be removed merely because it is humorous. Preserve humor when it is grounded in the subject, fits the surrounding voice, reveals self-awareness, helps the reader recognize a shared frustration, creates useful contrast or relief, or does not weaken clarity or precision.
